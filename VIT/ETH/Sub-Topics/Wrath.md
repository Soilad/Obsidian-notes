# Tumbler Ridge and OpenAI: Is AI Good or Bad for Society?

_Note: The WSJ and CBC pages blocked direct access, so these points come from other outlets that covered the WSJ report and the shooting._

## What happened

- **The attack:** On February 10, 2026, eight people were killed in Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia. Five of them were children between the ages of 12 and 13. More than 25 were injured, and the 18-year-old suspect was found dead at the scene.
- **The warning signs:** The shooter's posts about gun violence were flagged by OpenAI's automatic review systems in June 2025. Around a dozen employees reportedly knew about the concerning interactions months before the attack.
- **The decision:** Several employees worried the posts could lead to real-world violence and urged leaders to contact authorities, but leadership declined. The account was banned for violating the usage policy, but not reported to police.
- **The loophole:** After the shooter's name was released, OpenAI found the shooter had used a second ChatGPT account, which it then shared with police.
- **Afterward:** OpenAI contacted the RCMP only after the shooting. British Columbia has since sued OpenAI and Sam Altman, alleging negligence and an unsafe product. These claims have not yet been tested in court.

## How AI looks good here

- The automated system worked. It caught the violent content and sent it to human review.
- OpenAI's caution had a reason. It said over-reporting can cause distress to a young person and family if officers show up unannounced, and raises privacy concerns.
- The company changed course. It made its referral threshold more flexible, since users may not discuss the target, means, and timing of planned violence.

## How AI looks bad here

- Detection didn't lead to prevention. The company knew, and the information stayed inside the company.
- A ban did little. The user simply made a new account.
- A private company set the rules on its own for when a threat counts as real. No law required it to report.

## Ethical frameworks

### Virtue ethics (What would a person of good character do?)

- **Verdict: Unethical.**
- Employees who raised alarms showed courage and care. Leadership did not.
- Prudence (practical wisdom) means acting on a reasonable fear when lives are at stake, not waiting for proof of a date and target.
- Altman's apology shows some later honesty, but virtue is judged by what you do when it matters.

### Deontology (Did they follow their duties?)

- **Verdict: Mixed, leaning unethical.**
- OpenAI followed its own rule exactly. In a strict, rule-following sense, it did its duty.
- But Kant also asks whether the rule itself could be a universal law. A rule that lets a company sit on knowledge of likely violence fails that test.
- There is a real duty on the other side: respecting user privacy and not treating every user as a suspect. That duty is real, but it is weaker than the duty to protect life.

### Utilitarianism (Which choice leads to the best outcome for the most people?)

- **Verdict: Unethical in outcome.**
- The cost of reporting would have been small: maybe an awkward police visit or a privacy complaint.
- The cost of not reporting was eight deaths and more than 25 injuries.
- A fair counterpoint: a rule-utilitarian might say mass over-reporting would harm many innocent users. Even so, this case had a dozen staff raising concerns, which is a strong signal, not a random flag.

## Key quotes

- OpenAI's threshold for calling police: "an imminent and credible risk of serious physical harm to others"
- Sam Altman, in his apology letter: "deeply sorry that we did not alert law enforcement"
- OpenAI VP Ann O'Leary, admitting the policy has changed: "we would refer the account banned in June 2025 to law enforcement" if it were found today.
- B.C. Premier David Eby: "profoundly disturbing for the victims' families and all British Columbians"
- Eby on OpenAI's new promises: "cold comfort for the people of Tumbler Ridge"

## Bottom line

AI was not the problem by itself. It detected the danger. The failure was a human decision about what to do with that warning. This case makes the argument for clear, legal reporting standards for AI companies, which Eby has asked the federal government to create.
